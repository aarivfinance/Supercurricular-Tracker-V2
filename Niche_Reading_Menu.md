# Niche Reading Menu — Philosophy, Politics, Economics, Law
### Multiple specialist options per sub-topic. Pick what matches your actual interest or essay question — you don't need to read all of these, and you shouldn't try to.

A note on difficulty: everything here is genuinely niche/advanced by design, since that's what you asked for. Some (marked **[hard]**) are dense academic texts — fine to read partially and be honest about that at interview ("I read the first three chapters of X and found...") rather than claim you finished something you skimmed.

---

## PHILOSOPHY

### Ethics & Moral Philosophy
- **Bernard Williams**, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy* — sharp critique of systematic moral theories (utilitarianism, Kantianism); good if you want to argue *against* neat ethical systems rather than for one.
- **Alasdair MacIntyre**, *After Virtue* **[hard]** — revives Aristotelian virtue ethics as a critique of Enlightenment moral philosophy; genuinely distinctive, rarely seen on applicant reading lists.
- **Derek Parfit**, *Reasons and Persons* **[hard]** — connects personal identity to ethics; extremely influential in academic philosophy, extremely difficult. Read selectively.
- **Philippa Foot**, *Virtues and Vices* — the philosopher who invented the trolley problem; short essays, more approachable than the above.
- **Peter Singer**, *Practical Ethics* — applied/utilitarian, deliberately provocative (animal rights, effective altruism, bioethics); good if you want to argue with a position rather than just describe it.

### Political Philosophy & Justice (beyond Rawls/Nozick)
- **G.A. Cohen**, *Why Not Socialism?* — short (under 100 pages), sharp egalitarian critique of markets; a genuinely accessible entry into a "hard" thinker.
- **Michael Walzer**, *Spheres of Justice* — argues against Rawls' single-currency theory of justice, proposing that different goods (money, office, education) should be distributed by different principles.
- **Iris Marion Young**, *Justice and the Politics of Difference* — critiques the whole "distributive" paradigm of justice, focusing on oppression and structural inequality instead.
- **Charles Taylor**, *Sources of the Self* **[hard]** — communitarian critique of liberal individualism; dense but a genuinely distinctive counterpoint to Rawls/Nozick-style liberalism.

### Epistemology & Philosophy of Mind
- **Edmund Gettier**, *"Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?"* — a 3-page paper, not a book, that reshaped modern epistemology; genuinely readable in one sitting and a strong interview talking point.
- **Thomas Nagel**, *"What Is It Like to Be a Bat?"* (essay) — short, foundational to the philosophy of consciousness.
- **David Chalmers**, *The Conscious Mind* **[hard]** — formalises the "hard problem of consciousness"; more technical than Nagel's essay.
- **Daniel Dennett**, *Consciousness Explained* — takes the opposite position to Chalmers (consciousness is fully explicable physically); **pair the two** for a live debate.

### Philosophy of Science & Logic
- **Thomas Kuhn**, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* — "paradigm shifts"; hugely influential across every discipline, not just philosophy — strong PPE-crossover value since it applies to how economics and political science think of themselves as sciences.
- **Karl Popper**, *Conjectures and Refutations* — falsifiability as the criterion of science; **pair with Kuhn** for a genuine live debate about how scientific knowledge actually progresses.

## POLITICS

### Political Theory & Ideology (beyond Mill/Rousseau/Marx)
- **Hannah Arendt**, *The Origins of Totalitarianism* **[hard]** — dense but among the most-cited 20th-century political theory texts; also available as smaller standalone essays if the full book is too much.
- **Antonio Gramsci**, selections from the *Prison Notebooks* — "cultural hegemony"; explains how power operates through culture and ideas, not just institutions.
- **Robert Dahl**, *Polyarchy* — more technical/political-science than philosophical; a rigorous account of what makes a system actually democratic in practice, not just on paper.
- **Carl Schmitt**, *The Concept of the Political* — the "friend/enemy" theory of politics. Genuinely influential and widely taught, but flag honestly: Schmitt was a Nazi party member, and his work is studied *critically*, not endorsed — worth being explicit about that distinction if you cite him.

### International Relations & Global Order
- **John Mearsheimer**, *The Tragedy of Great Power Politics* — realist IR theory: states pursue power because the international system is anarchic.
- **Alexander Wendt**, *Social Theory of International Politics* **[hard]** — constructivist counter to Mearsheimer: state interests are socially constructed, not fixed. **Pair the two** for a genuine IR-theory debate.
- **Robert Keohane**, *After Hegemony* — institutionalist alternative: cooperation between states is possible even under anarchy, via institutions.
- **Samuel Huntington**, *The Clash of Civilizations* — controversial, widely critiqued, but genuinely influential; good if you want to engage with (and push back on) a provocative thesis.

### Comparative Politics & Democracy
- **Robert Dahl**, *On Democracy* — more accessible than *Polyarchy*, a good starting point on what democracy actually requires institutionally.
- **Steven Levitsky & Daniel Ziblatt**, *How Democracies Die* — contemporary, very readable, on how democracies erode from within rather than via coups — strong current-affairs linking material.

### Political Economy
- **Karl Polanyi**, *The Great Transformation* **[hard]** — argues markets are always politically constructed, never "natural"; historically grounded critique of market society.
- **Daron Acemoglu & James Robinson**, *Why Nations Fail* — institutional economics/politics crossover (also listed under Economics below — it genuinely straddles both).

## ECONOMICS

### Behavioural Economics
- **Daniel Kahneman**, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* — foundational; long, but the two-systems framework (fast/intuitive vs. slow/deliberate thinking) is worth knowing properly, not just by reputation.
- **Richard Thaler**, *Misbehaving: The Making of Behavioral Economics* — a memoir/history of the field's rise; more narrative and personal than Kahneman, good if you want the "how the field developed" angle.
- **Dan Ariely**, *Predictably Irrational* — accessible, experiment-driven, easier entry point than Kahneman.

### Development Economics
- **Banerjee & Duflo**, *Poor Economics* — randomised-trial approach to poverty (already a strong pick if you haven't read it yet).
- **William Easterly**, *The White Man's Burden* — sharply critical of large-scale foreign aid, arguing it often does more harm than good.
- **Jeffrey Sachs**, *The End of Poverty* — takes the opposite position: aid, properly targeted, can end extreme poverty. **Pair Easterly and Sachs** — this is a genuinely live, unresolved debate in development economics.
- **Dambisa Moyo**, *Dead Aid* — a Zambian economist's critique of aid dependency; distinctive because it's argued from an African-development perspective rather than a Western donor one.

### Economic History
- **Niall Ferguson**, *The Ascent of Money* — accessible history of finance and banking across centuries.
- **Sven Beckert**, *Empire of Cotton* **[hard]** — global economic history told through the cotton trade; genuinely distinctive angle, rarely on generic reading lists.
- **Robert Allen**, *Global Economic History: A Very Short Introduction* — short, dense, good if you want grounding without committing to a 500-page book.

### Macroeconomics & Monetary Policy
- **Adam Tooze**, *Crashed: How a Decade of Financial Crises Changed the World* — the 2008 crisis and its long aftermath; highly regarded, genuinely current in its relevance.
- **Ben Bernanke**, *The Courage to Act* — insider account of the 2008 crisis from the then-Fed Chair; good if you want a policymaker's perspective rather than an academic's.
- **Milton Friedman & Anna Schwartz**, *A Monetary History of the United States* **[hard]** — foundational to monetarism; very technical, read selectively (the Great Depression chapters are the most cited).

### Inequality & Political Economy
- **Piketty**, *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* (already a strong pick if new to you).
- **Branko Milanovic**, *Global Inequality* — a genuinely different lens from Piketty: focuses on inequality *between* countries as much as within them.
- **Anthony Atkinson**, *Inequality: What Can Be Done?* — more policy-focused and prescriptive than Piketty's historical approach.

### Free-Market & Heterodox Economics
- **Hayek**, *The Road to Serfdom* / **Friedman**, *Capitalism and Freedom* (already strong picks if new to you).
- **Ha-Joon Chang**, *Kicking Away the Ladder* — more academic and historically argued than his popular *23 Things*; critiques free-trade orthodoxy using the actual historical record of how rich countries developed.
- **Naomi Klein**, *The Shock Doctrine* — polemical, critical of neoliberalism; useful for showing you've engaged with the critique, not just the mainstream position.

## LAW

### Jurisprudence & Legal Theory (beyond Hart/Dworkin)
- **Joseph Raz**, *The Authority of Law* **[hard]** — develops legal positivism further than Hart; genuinely advanced.
- **John Finnis**, *Natural Law and Natural Rights* **[hard]** — modern natural law theory, a direct counter-tradition to positivism (Hart/Raz); **pair with either** for a positivism vs. natural law debate.
- **Jeremy Waldron**, *Law and Disagreement* — connects legal theory to democratic theory: how should law handle genuine moral disagreement in society?

### Human Rights & Civil Liberties
- **Conor Gearty**, *Can Human Rights Survive?* — critical but sympathetic look at the human rights framework's real-world limits.
- **Michael Ignatieff**, *Human Rights as Politics and Idolatry* — argues human rights should be understood as a political practice, not a quasi-religious ideal — a distinctive, slightly contrarian angle.

### Criminal Justice
- **Michelle Alexander**, *The New Jim Crow* — US-focused (mass incarceration and race), but hugely influential and useful comparative material against the UK criminal justice system.
- **Nils Christie**, *Crime Control as Industry* — short, critical of the punishment system as a self-perpetuating industry rather than a rational response to crime.

### Constitutional & Public Law
- **A.V. Dicey**, *Introduction to the Study of the Law of the Constitution* **[hard, but foundational]** — the classic source of "parliamentary sovereignty" and "the rule of law" as UK constitutional principles; genuinely worth knowing directly rather than by reputation.
- **Vernon Bogdanor**, *The New British Constitution* — more contemporary and readable, covering how the UK's uncodified constitution has actually evolved (devolution, human rights, EU/Brexit).

### Commercial & Financial Law
- **Walter Bagehot**, *Lombard Street* (already a strong pick — the historical root of central-banking regulation).
- **John Kay**, *Other People's Money* — contemporary critique of how modern finance actually works versus what it's supposed to do.

## How to use this without drowning in choice

Pick **one sub-topic per subject** that genuinely interests you — not one from every row. Read one accessible entry and one harder one within that sub-topic, and use the contrast (not the total number of books) as your personal-statement material. Depth in one or two sub-topics beats a scattered list across all of them.
